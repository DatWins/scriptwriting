*A practical screenwriting guide*

**Source discussion:** [Open the original Reddit post](https://www.reddit.com/r/Screenwriting/comments/1x9mmu6/how_do_i_start_a_new_scene_in_the_same_location/)

This guide expands the discussion into practical formatting choices and examples. The original question concerns two people stuck in an elevator while time passes.

## The quick answer

If the location stays the same but meaningful time passes, you can begin a new scene with a new scene heading and a time marker such as LATER or 30 MINUTES LATER. Choose the clearest format for the reader; you usually do not need a separate CUT TO: just to announce the change.

## 1. Choose the simplest clear format

Use a fresh scene heading when the story has made a meaningful jump in time, even if the characters are still in the same physical place.

**Example — general time jump:**

```
INT. SHANGRI-LA HOTEL – ELEVATOR – DAY

Carl presses the emergency button. Nothing happens.

INT. SHANGRI-LA HOTEL – ELEVATOR – LATER

The overhead light flickers. Carol sits on the floor, conserving her phone battery.
```

**Example — precise time jump:**

```
INT. SHANGRI-LA HOTEL – ELEVATOR – DAY

They try every button. The elevator doesn't move.

INT. SHANGRI-LA HOTEL – ELEVATOR – 30 MINUTES LATER

Carl's jacket is balled up beneath Carol's head. His phone reads 2% battery.
```

## 2. When to use LATER

Use LATER when the exact amount of elapsed time is not important to the audience. It signals a clear time shift without cluttering the page with unnecessary precision.

Use a specific marker such as 30 MINUTES LATER when the duration matters to the story—for example, when a rescue deadline, a dwindling phone battery, or a character's patience depends on the passage of time.

## 3. When a mini-slug can work

A mini-slug is a short internal heading used within a larger scene or sequence. It can be useful when you want to direct attention to a distinct beat without repeating a full scene heading. Formatting conventions vary, so prioritize clarity and consistency.

**Example:**

```
INT. HOTEL ELEVATOR – DAY

Carl jams his fingers between the doors. They don't budge.

THIRTY MINUTES LATER

They sit on opposite sides of the elevator, no longer speaking.
```

## 4. Sometimes, show time passing instead

If the elapsed time itself is dramatic, make it visible. A new heading tells the reader that time has passed; action can make the audience feel that passage.

For the trapped-elevator example, you might show the characters exhausting their options, taking turns pressing the call button, watching a phone battery drain, growing hungry, or shifting from frantic effort to exhausted silence.

**Example:**

```
INT. HOTEL ELEVATOR – DAY

Carl presses the alarm. A tinny buzz answers.

Carol calls for help. Her voice echoes back.

They try the doors together. Nothing.

Later, Carl sits with his head against the wall. Carol's phone dies in her hand.

                    CARL
          How long has it been?

                    CAROL
          Long enough.
```

## 5. Do you need CUT TO:?

Usually, no. A new scene heading already communicates the scene change. CUT TO: is available when a particular transition deserves emphasis, but adding it to every scene break can make a spec screenplay feel over-directed.

## 6. Is it a new scene if the location is identical?

Often, yes: a scene is commonly understood as action occurring in one place at one time. A meaningful time jump can justify a new scene heading even when the location remains unchanged. A continuous stretch of action in the same place and time generally stays under the same heading.

There is no need to split every small beat into a new scene. The goal is to make time, place, and the flow of action easy to follow for both the reader and production team.

## 7. Common mistakes to avoid

- Repeating a full heading without making clear what changed.
- Using an exact time jump when the duration has no story value.
- Writing CUT TO: automatically at every scene change.
- Making readers infer a major time jump from vague action alone.
- Over-explaining elapsed time instead of letting a strong visual detail communicate it.

## 8. A simple decision rule

Ask yourself three questions:

1. Has meaningful time passed?
2. Does the audience need to know how much?
3. Is the passage of time itself dramatic?

If time has passed but the amount is unimportant, use LATER. If the amount matters, specify it. If experiencing the wait matters, show a few telling changes in behavior or the environment. You can combine a clear time marker with visual action.

## 9. Quick practice

Write the same elevator moment three ways:

- **A.** A new heading ending in LATER.
- **B.** A new heading ending in 30 MINUTES LATER.
- **C.** One continuous scene where action shows the wait passing.

Compare them. Which version is easiest to picture? Which makes the time jump most emotionally effective? Keep the version that best serves the story.

## Takeaway

Make the time jump unmistakable, but don't make the formatting do more work than necessary. A clean new scene heading with LATER or a specific time marker is often enough. When the wait matters emotionally, pair that marker with visual action that shows what the characters have endured.
