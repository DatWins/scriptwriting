## The Core Question

A new screenwriter has completed one screenplay and usually writes thrillers. They have heard that one screenplay page roughly equals one minute of screen time, and they struggle to build ideas into a feature-length script. They can imagine an opening and an ending, but often get stuck around 30 pages. They want to improve dialogue, tension, creativity, and their overall writing.

## Condensed Answers — Expanded Explanation

### 1. How do I make a screenplay longer?

Develop the story rather than padding the page count. Length is a result of how much meaningful story the film needs, not a goal to reach by adding extra conversations or repetitive scenes.

Ask whether the story has enough meaningful turns: Does the protagonist pursue a clear goal? Do obstacles complicate that goal? Do choices create consequences? Do relationships change? Does the situation become more difficult or reveal new information? If these elements are thin, the story may need development. If they are already strong, the idea may simply work better as a shorter film.

A feature does not have to reach an arbitrary number of pages. The familiar “one page equals one minute” rule is only a rough guideline; pace, action, dialogue, and formatting affect runtime.

### 2. Why don’t better dialogue and more tension automatically make the script longer?

Dialogue, tension, and length are related, but they are different craft problems. Better dialogue often becomes shorter because characters speak more precisely. Tension comes from uncertainty, conflict, stakes, and the possibility that something important may go wrong—not from making every scene loud or dramatic.

First determine what happens in the story and why. Then use dialogue to reveal character, negotiate conflict, hide information, change someone’s mind, or create a decision. Use tension when a character wants something and faces resistance, risk, or a difficult choice. Do not make every scene equally intense; quieter moments can give the audience room to breathe and make major moments land harder.

### 3. I have an opening and an ending, but how do I find the middle?

Outline the story before trying to write all the dialogue. Write down the opening situation, the ending, and one important event in the middle. Then fill the gaps by asking two questions repeatedly:

- “What must happen before this event can occur?”

- “What happens because this event occurred?”

For example, if the ending requires the protagonist to confront a former friend, establish why they became estranged, what brings them back into contact, why they cannot simply walk away, and what forces the confrontation to happen now. Each answer becomes a possible plot point. Repeat this process between every pair of major events, including the second half of the story.

The key is causality. “Then this happens, then that happens” can feel like a list. “Because this happens, the character makes a choice, which causes the next problem” feels like a story.

### 4. How do I know whether one event leads logically to the next?

For every major beat, identify the cause, the character’s response, and the consequence. A useful chain looks like this:

1\. The protagonist wants something.  
2. An obstacle prevents them from getting it easily.  
3. They choose a strategy.  
4. That choice creates a new complication.  
5. The complication forces a harder choice.

If a scene can be removed without changing what follows, it may not be doing enough story work. It might still be valuable for atmosphere or character, but make that purpose intentional. Strong sequences tend to change the situation, deepen a relationship, reveal information, raise the stakes, or force a decision.

### 5. What should I do when I can’t think of enough scenes?

Build a deeper foundation for the characters and their world. For each important character, consider what they want, what they fear, what they believe, what they are hiding, what they need from other people, and what they would never willingly do. Then ask how those traits create conflict.

Backstory is useful when it affects present behavior. A character who distrusts authority might refuse help, make a problem worse, or misread another person’s intentions. A character who needs approval might agree to something they know is unwise. Those choices can create scenes naturally.

You do not need to keep every idea you invent. Early outlining is a workshop, not a contract. Generate possibilities, then keep the scenes that make the story clearer, more emotionally engaging, or more inevitable.

### 6. How can I make dialogue stronger?

Give each character a purpose in the conversation. They may be trying to persuade, avoid a question, get information, conceal a feeling, win an argument, or end the conversation. Their spoken words do not always have to match their real objective; that gap creates subtext.

Make voices distinct through priorities, word choice, rhythm, humor, directness, and what each character notices. Avoid having every character explain information they already know solely for the audience. Let important information emerge through disagreement, discovery, action, or a decision.

A useful revision test: What changes during this conversation? If nothing changes, consider shortening it, giving it a clearer dramatic purpose, or expressing the information visually.

### 7. How do I create tension in a thriller?

Tension grows when the audience understands what a character wants, what stands in the way, and what could be lost. Give the character incomplete information, limited time, conflicting loyalties, or a plan that depends on another person. Then let choices have consequences.

Vary the type and intensity of tension. A sequence might begin with a small uncertainty, develop into a misunderstanding, and end with a discovery that changes the plan. A quieter scene can still be tense if the character is hiding something or waiting for an answer that matters.

Avoid trying to make every moment suspenseful. Contrast is powerful: a calm scene can establish normality, reveal a vulnerability, or build an emotional bond that later events put under pressure.

### 8. How can I structure a feature as a series of sequences?

Think of the screenplay as a chain of connected sequences, each with a small dramatic arc. A sequence can have a goal, complications, a turning point, and a result that launches the next sequence.

For example, a protagonist tries to get access to a restricted meeting. The attempt fails, so they seek help from a reluctant colleague. That bargain creates a new obligation, which complicates the next attempt. The sequences feel distinct, but together they form one continuous story.

This approach makes a large project less overwhelming: you are not inventing 100 pages all at once. You are solving a series of smaller dramatic problems, each of which changes the conditions for what comes next.

### 9. How do I study screenplays and improve my instincts?

Read screenplays in the genre you want to write, then watch the corresponding films when possible. Notice where scenes begin and end, how quickly the central problem becomes clear, what information is withheld, how the protagonist’s goal changes, and how each sequence turns.

Compare what the script communicates through action, dialogue, and visual detail. Ask why a scene is included and what would be lost if it were removed. Studying several examples helps you notice patterns without treating any single screenplay as a formula that every story must follow.

You can also read novels and short stories for ideas about character and structure, but remember that screenplays must communicate primarily through what an audience can see and hear.

### 10. What if my idea is too small for a feature?

Consider whether the premise naturally contains enough conflict and change for a feature. Some ideas have a powerful central moment but not enough development for a long film. That does not make them bad ideas; they may be ideal for a short film.

Completing short scripts is valuable practice because it teaches you to establish a situation, create a turn, and deliver an ending. A finished short can teach more about structure than repeatedly expanding a premise that does not support a longer story.

If the idea does support a feature, look for meaningful escalation and character change—not unrelated subplots added solely to increase length.

### 11. What writing routine can help me finish?

Set a specific, manageable goal for each writing session. It could be a number of pages, a sequence outline, or a particular scene. The Reddit discussion suggests setting a concrete target and continuing even when the first draft is rough.

Separate drafting from revising when possible. During outlining, focus on cause and effect. During the first draft, keep moving and make notes when a problem needs more thought. During revision, test the structure, remove repetition, strengthen character choices, and sharpen dialogue.

Consistency helps you discover problems that are hard to solve by thinking about the story only occasionally. The goal is not to produce perfect pages every day; it is to create material you can evaluate and improve.

## A Practical Story-Building Worksheet

- **Opening:** What is the protagonist’s ordinary situation, and what do they want?

- **Disruption:** What event makes the old situation impossible to maintain?

- **First response:** What does the protagonist try first, and why does it fail or create a cost?

- **Middle turning point:** What discovery, reversal, or decision changes how the protagonist understands the problem?

- **Escalation:** What gets harder, and what new consequences follow from the protagonist’s choices?

- **Crisis:** What difficult choice forces the protagonist to risk something important?

- **Climax:** What action resolves the central conflict?

- **Resolution:** What has changed in the protagonist, their relationships, or their world?

## Scene Test: Does This Scene Earn Its Place?

- What does the point-of-view character want in this scene?

- Who or what stands in the way?

- What tactic does the character try?

- What changes by the end of the scene?

- What new problem, information, decision, or consequence leads into the next scene?

- Can the same purpose be achieved in a more visual or concise way?

## Key Takeaway

Do not begin by asking, “How do I reach 100 pages?” Begin by asking, “What is the next meaningful event, why does it happen, and how does it change what the character must do?” Build a causal chain of events, give characters clear motivations, and make each sequence alter the story. Length should emerge from a complete and engaging story—not from padding.
