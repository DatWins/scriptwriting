**Original Reddit discussion:** [How to screenwrite a TV show? — r/scriptwriting](https://www.reddit.com/r/scriptwriting/comments/1x9vcsg/how_to_screenwrite_a_tv_show/)

### The Original Question

The poster has imagined a complete, one-season TV story over roughly two years, with a definite ending, but does not know how to begin writing it. They ask for templates, courses, guidelines, and practical experience with writing or selling a TV show.

## 1. Where should I start if I have a TV series idea but have never written a television script?

Start by turning the idea in your head into a small set of written documents. Begin with a one-sentence premise: who the story follows, what they want, what stands in their way, and what makes the situation compelling over time. Then write a short series overview that explains the central characters, the world, the main conflict, and the kind of stories the show can tell.

Next, decide what you want to write first. For most new writers, the most useful starting point is a pilot episode: the first episode that introduces the show and demonstrates how it works. You do not need to write every episode before you learn the format. However, if you already envision a limited series with a definite ending, a brief episode-by-episode outline can help show how the story develops across the season.

## 2. What is a TV pilot, and what does it need to accomplish?

A pilot is both an episode and a demonstration of the series. It introduces the protagonist, the central situation, key relationships, tone, and the main source of conflict. It should give the audience a satisfying episode-level experience while making them curious about what comes next.

A common beginner mistake is trying to explain the entire backstory or summarize the whole season in the pilot. Instead, dramatize the moment when the characters’ situation becomes interesting. Give the protagonist a goal, put obstacles in the way, and end with a meaningful change, discovery, choice, or unresolved question.

A pilot should also demonstrate the repeatable engine of the show: the kind of conflict or story situation that can generate future episodes. In a limited series, that engine may be the unfolding of one central story rather than a new case or problem every week.

## 3. How do I decide whether my idea is a limited series or an ongoing show?

A limited series is designed around a contained story with a planned progression and ending. It often has a set number of episodes, and each installment moves the central narrative closer to its resolution. An ongoing series needs a durable story engine: a workplace, family, community, investigation, competition, or other situation that can produce new conflicts while the characters continue to change.

Ask yourself: What keeps generating story after the pilot? What new problem can arise without feeling repetitive? What changes from episode to episode? If the core appeal is a single relationship or event that naturally leads to one definitive ending, a limited series may be the right fit. If the characters’ world can keep producing fresh conflicts and choices, an ongoing format may work better.

Neither format is inherently better. Choose the one that best serves the story, rather than stretching or shrinking the premise to fit a trend.

## 4. How long should a TV script be?

Length depends on the format, genre, broadcaster, platform, and production style. As rough starting points, the Reddit discussion mentions about 28–32 pages for a half-hour single-camera script and 58–62 pages for an hour-long drama. These are not universal rules: comedies, dramas, animation, streaming series, and individual production requirements vary.

The page-to-minute rule is only a rough estimate. Read scripts for shows with a similar format and compare them with the finished episodes. Use the conventions of the specific type of show you are writing, and prioritize clear, well-paced storytelling over hitting a number exactly.

## 5. What is teleplay format, and what software should I use?

A teleplay is a script written for television, using standard screenplay elements such as scene headings, action, character names, dialogue, and transitions where appropriate. Professional screenwriting software helps format these elements consistently, so you can focus on the story instead of manually spacing every line.

The Reddit commenters recommend using screenwriting software with television templates. Some tools are paid and some are free; choose one that supports the format you need and lets you export a clean PDF. Before sharing a script, check that scene headings, character cues, dialogue, page breaks, and title information are formatted consistently.

Formatting is not a substitute for storytelling, but professional, readable pages make the script easier for others to evaluate.

## 6. Do I need act breaks, a teaser, or a cold open?

It depends on the intended format and outlet. Traditional ad-supported broadcast shows often use a teaser followed by several acts, with act-outs designed to keep viewers interested through commercial breaks. Streaming scripts may not label act breaks in the same way, but thinking in acts can still help you organize escalation and turning points.

A teaser is a short opening sequence that hooks the audience and establishes tone, character, or the central problem. In a drama, it may set the main story in motion or introduce a mystery. In comedy, a cold open may deliver a quick comic situation or introduce the characters before the main episode begins.

Do not add act labels just because a template says to. Study scripts from shows that resemble yours and follow the conventions that fit the intended format.

## 7. How should I structure the pilot episode?

A useful pilot outline can be built from a few core movements: introduce the protagonist and their normal world; disrupt that world; show the protagonist reacting and making choices; complicate the situation; and finish with a turn that makes the next episode feel worth watching.

For an act-structured show, each act should generally end with a question, reversal, revelation, or decision that creates momentum. A cliffhanger does not always need to be a huge surprise. It can be a new piece of information, a relationship changing, a plan going wrong, or a choice the character can no longer avoid.

For a streaming show without labeled act breaks, the same principle applies at the sequence level: keep changing the situation and give the audience reasons to continue.

## 8. How do I balance the main story and secondary storylines?

Keep the protagonist and the central story in focus, especially in the pilot. Secondary characters and B-stories can add contrast, humor, emotional depth, or complications, but too many scenes away from the main story can make the episode feel scattered.

A B-story should usually connect thematically or causally to the A-story, even if it follows different characters. It might mirror the protagonist’s dilemma, reveal a side of a relationship, or create a complication that affects the main plot later. Ask what each storyline contributes and how it changes the characters or audience’s understanding.

In a pilot, introduce only the secondary characters and threads the audience can follow clearly. You can expand the ensemble as the series develops.

## 9. How can I learn pacing and scene construction from existing shows?

The Reddit discussion recommends watching the pilot of a comparable show with a notebook and studying its construction. Count the scenes, estimate their lengths, note which characters appear, and mark where major turns, surprises, jokes, or shifts in storyline happen. Notice how the episode moves between plotlines and how each scene changes the situation.

Then read the screenplay if you can find a legitimate copy. Compare the written scene with what appears on screen. Notice what the script conveys through action rather than dialogue, how quickly scenes begin, and where they end. This is not about copying another show; it is about learning the rhythm and conventions of the format you want to write.

Choose more than one comparison show so you learn a range of approaches rather than assuming one pilot is a universal template.

## 10. Should I write all the episodes before pitching the show?

There is no single answer for every project. A clear pilot and a concise series overview are often a practical first package for developing the concept. For a limited series, an episode-by-episode outline can help demonstrate that the story has a coherent path and ending. Drafting more episodes can also help you test whether the premise sustains its momentum.

The Reddit thread includes one commenter who is drafting all nine episodes before pitching. That is an individual approach, not a universal industry requirement. Consider your goals, time, and the feedback you can get. You may learn more by revising a strong pilot and outline than by drafting an entire season before discovering a structural problem.

Before submitting anything, research the current expectations of the specific producer, program, competition, or representative you intend to approach.

## 11. When should I show my script to people in the industry?

One commenter advises beginners not to rush their earliest scripts to industry professionals, because a first draft may not represent their actual ability. A sensible takeaway is to develop the work before making high-stakes submissions: complete a draft, revise it, check the format, and seek thoughtful feedback from readers who understand screenwriting.

Feedback should help you identify what is clear, confusing, compelling, or underdeveloped. Do not make every change just because one person suggests it; look for patterns across readers and decide which notes serve the story. A polished sample gives others a better sense of your voice and craft.

You do not need to wait until you feel perfectly ready to share work with trusted peers. The point is to use feedback as part of the learning process and to be selective about formal submissions.

## 12. What courses and learning resources are worth exploring?

The Reddit commenters suggest reading widely in screenwriting communities, studying produced scripts, using software templates, and checking out the free introductory course and podcasts from Young Screenwriters mentioned in the thread. These are starting points, not endorsements or guarantees of a particular outcome.

A practical learning loop is: study a script, watch the episode, outline its structure, write your own scene or pilot, and revise it after feedback. Focus on applying one lesson at a time—such as clearer scene goals, stronger act-outs, more visual storytelling, or more distinct character voices—rather than collecting endless advice without writing.

## 13. How should I plan a limited series with a definite ending?

Write down the ending you are aiming for and the major changes that must happen before it feels earned. Then map the season as a sequence of turning points: what the protagonist learns, what choices they make, how relationships shift, and how each episode changes the central problem.

For every episode, identify its main dramatic question, the protagonist’s goal, the biggest obstacle, the turning point, and the new situation at the end. Episodes should feel satisfying in their own right while also contributing to the larger arc. Avoid making episodes feel like arbitrary slices of one long movie; each installment needs its own shape and momentum.

Keep the plan flexible. An outline is a guide for discovering the story, not a promise that every beat must remain unchanged.

## 14. What are the most common beginner mistakes to avoid?

Common pitfalls include explaining too much backstory, introducing too many characters and plotlines at once, relying on dialogue to explain everything, and writing scenes that do not change the situation. Another is polishing a premise endlessly without completing a script.

The thread also stresses proofreading the first page carefully. Typos and inconsistent formatting can distract a reader before the story has a chance to make an impression. Read the script aloud or use a text-to-speech pass, check scene headings and character names, and proofread a final exported copy.

Above all, finish drafts. You will learn different lessons from a completed pilot than from a concept that exists only in your head.

## A Practical Roadmap

1.  **Step 1 — Define the show:** Write a logline, genre, tone, format (half-hour or hour-long), and whether it is limited or ongoing.
2.  **Step 2 — Describe the engine:** Explain what repeatedly creates conflict and generates episodes.
3.  **Step 3 — Map the season:** For a limited series, list the major turning points and intended ending; for an ongoing show, define the recurring story engine and character arcs.
4.  **Step 4 — Outline the pilot:** Identify the opening hook, protagonist’s goal, disruption, complications, key choice, and ending turn.
5.  **Step 5 — Study comparable pilots:** Read scripts and watch episodes; note scene rhythm, act-outs, character introductions, and storylines.
6.  **Step 6 — Draft in proper format:** Use screenwriting software with a television template and concentrate on visual, playable scenes.
7.  **Step 7 — Revise and get feedback:** Strengthen clarity, pace, character goals, scene turns, and the final hook; proofread before sharing.

## Scene Checklist

- What does the character want in this scene?
- What obstacle or opposing desire gets in the way?
- What new information, action, or decision changes the situation?
- How does the scene move the A-story or a purposeful B-story forward?
- Where does the scene begin and end—and can it be tighter?
- What question or consequence pulls the audience into the next scene?

## Key Takeaway

Start by writing a pilot, not by trying to solve the entire television industry at once. Define the series engine, study comparable scripts and episodes, use a proper teleplay format, and revise the pilot until it clearly introduces the characters, tone, central conflict, and reason to keep watching. For a limited series, support the pilot with a concise season outline that shows how the story reaches its planned ending.
